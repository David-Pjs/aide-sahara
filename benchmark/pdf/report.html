Does Sahara hear Nigerian code-switched speech better than the models developers reach for?

Aide benchmark report · Sahara CodeSwitch Africa Challenge · David Uhumagho and Dillon Ofili · September 2026 · Code, transcripts and scripts: github.com/David-Pjs/aide-sahara

1. Summary

Across two Intron datasets and four Nigerian language pairs, Sahara v2.5 had the lowest word error rate overall on both sets (46.1% on long conversations, 54.7% on short clips), produced no runaway hallucination loop and no non-Latin script, and kept far more of the Nigerian-language words inside code-switched sentences. It is not best everywhere: it loses the most English-heavy conversation and the short Yoruba clips, and it is the slowest.

2. Data

SetSource and licenceLanguagesClipsAudioDomain and recording
AfriSwitchCarehuggingface.co/datasets/intronhealth/AfriSwitchCare, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0Igbo, Yoruba, Hausa, Nigerian Pidgin, each with English418.6 minSimulated doctor and patient consultations, no real patients; studio, 16 kHz mono, clean; 1.5 to 6.3 min each
AfriSwitchhuggingface.co/datasets/intronhealth/AfriSwitch, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0The same four pairs, 5 clips each203.1 minNatural broadcast and conversational speech, uncontrolled noise; 4.6 to 15.3 s each

Selection. AfriSwitch clips were chosen deterministically: 4 to 20 s long, at least 2 switch points, one clip per source recording, ranked by code-mix index. Preprocessing. (1) The Hausa AfriSwitch files declare 16 kHz but hold 48 kHz (4 files) or 44.1 kHz (1); confirmed by speaking rate against the dataset's durations and by voice pitch, then resampled by exact rational ratio to 16 kHz. Unrepaired, speech plays about three times too slow and every model's Hausa score measures a broken file. (2) Speaker-turn separators and one reference's written speaker labels ("[Spaeker 1]") were removed from references. (3) Reference typos ("7thingera") were left in place: a guessed correction puts our judgment in the answer key, and a typo costs every model equally. (4) Both sides are normalised with Intron's published pipeline before scoring. The two sets are never averaged together.

3. Metrics and why they fit

MetricDefinitionWhy it matters here
WER, CEREdits over reference words or characters, after Intron's normalisationStandard, comparable with Intron's own results; CER credits near-misses in agglutinative words
Accuracy, transcript lossCorrect words, and deleted words, over reference wordsSeparates "wrong" from "missing"; stays readable when WER exceeds 100%
Segment lossSentences with under 20% of words survivingA lost sentence is an instruction the agent never received
HallucinationInsertion rate plus a runaway-loop detector (10+ repeats)A blind user cannot see invented text; fluent errors are the dangerous ones
Code-switched span recallWords kept, split by the dataset's own [[EN]] tagsMeasures the switch itself, not the whole clip
Key-fact recall (downstream)Share of reference facts still stated in the transcript, judged by the LLM that drives AideAide's transcript goes straight to an agent; what matters is what it can still act on

All word metrics come from one alignment per transcript (src/metrics.ts). A fairness guard averages only clips every model scored. Averages carry 95% bootstrap intervals (10,000 resamples, fixed seed). Sahara received its API's per-clip language hint; Whisper ran raw (no prompt, no language, temperature 0) because it has no code for Igbo, Pidgin or mixed speech.

5. Qualitative findings per model and language

Sahara v2.5

  • Hausa: best on both sets by a wide margin; near-verbatim on short clips (hausa-3: 12.5% WER).
  • Pidgin: best long conversation (25.6%); loses 1 of 18 sentences. Minor 4x "yes" repeat, plausibly real speech.
  • Yoruba: keeps producing both languages through switch points in the long conversation; on short clips writes correct tone marks the references omit, but dropped words on some clips (2 words for a 14-word reference).
  • Igbo: weakest where speech is mostly fast clinical English (last of four); faithful rather than invented errors.

Whisper large-v3

  • Pidgin: 136-word "mwenye" loop, 22.4% of its output; on short Pidgin, wrote one clip in Bengali script.
  • Yoruba: 72x nonsense loop in the long conversation; best of three on short Yoruba under strict WER.
  • Hausa: renders speech as invented syllables ("E nao ni? A fia." for "Ina wuni lafiya"); fluent again once English resumes.
  • Igbo: good on the English, but invented a Q&A ("My name is Ruslan Abayisi") and dropped all tagged Igbo words on short clips.

Whisper large-v3-turbo

  • Hausa: translates instead of transcribing: "We have to do this." for a 32-word Hausa reference; Cyrillic output on one clip.
  • Yoruba: 85.2% on the long conversation, losing 50 of 66 sentences; repeated "We can take a photo of your knee" dozens of times.
  • Pidgin: competitive on short clips (62.2%); recovers the English well.
  • Igbo: second best on the long conversation; near zero Igbo words kept on short clips.

Qwen3-ASR-1.7B (long set only)

  • Igbo: wins outright on every metric; strongest on the least code-switched clip.
  • Pidgin: 33.4%, loses no sentence; outputs something for every stretch of audio.
  • Hausa: collapses (93.9%), writing Swahili-like words such as "shukrani", "asibiti".
  • Yoruba: close to Sahara (61.1% vs 58.5%) without loops.

6. Limitations

  • Small samples: 4 conversations and 20 clips; intervals above state how far that goes, and per-language figures are indicative.
  • Hosts and coverage: short-clip Whisper ran on Groq (same open weights, labelled); Qwen3-ASR is absent there because inference credit ran out.
  • Hausa short clips barely code-switch (code-mix index 6.25 vs 50.0 for the others) yet hold most Nigerian-language words in the span table.
  • Key-fact recall uses an LLM judge, the same model for every transcript; it measures what an agent can extract, not human understanding.
  • Benchmark audio is not Aide users' audio: real commands are short, on personal phones, in real rooms. No human listening evaluation yet.
